# Why Nordix Uses PolyForm Noncommercial

## The Changing Linux Landscape

The Linux desktop market is entering a new era. Lenovo, ASUS, HP, and Dell have all publicly announced increased investment in Linux-based laptops and desktops - driven by growing user dissatisfaction with the direction of proprietary operating systems. Systems like Ubuntu and Linux Mint are being shipped pre-installed, and with the maturity of Flatpak and native repositories, the software experience is now seamless enough for everyday users.

This is not a trend — this is Linux taking its place in the global desktop and laptop market.

Large, well-established distributions like Ubuntu and Mint will benefit from this shift. They have the backing, the infrastructure, and the sponsorship deals that come with being the default choice for major hardware vendors. That is a good thing for the Linux ecosystem as a whole.

But it also raises a question that every independent developer needs to ask themselves:

**If a company finds value in what I've built - what happens to me?**

## Why Not GPL?

Nordix was originally intended to be licensed under GPL v3. It's a well-respected license, and for many projects it makes perfect sense.

But GPL was designed in an era before the world became dependent on digital services. It protects the user's freedom to access and modify source code — and that is important. However, GPL does not protect the individual developer.

Under GPL, any company can take your work, build a product around it, ship it on hardware, or run it as a service — and you, the person who created it, see nothing. No credit, no compensation, no conversation. This is not a theoretical risk. It's exactly what has happened to projects like Elasticsearch and Redis, where the original creators eventually had to change their licenses after large corporations built profitable services on top of their work.

For a well-funded project with corporate sponsors, GPL works fine. For a solo developer with no income — GPL protects everyone except the person who wrote the code.

## What PolyForm Noncommercial Actually Means

PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0 is simple:

- **You can** use Nordix and Yggdrasil freely for personal and non-commercial purposes.
- **You can** read, study, modify, and learn from the source code.
- **You can** contribute to the project.
- **You can** share it with others for non-commercial use.

The **only** restriction compared to GPL is this: if a company wants to use Nordix commercially — to sell it, bundle it with hardware, or build a paid product around it — they need to have a conversation with the project first.

That's it. For the community, the day-to-day experience is identical to what GPL would offer.

## Why This Is Better for the Community — Not Worse

Some may see "non-commercial" and think it limits the project. The opposite is true.

Under GPL, if a company found Nordix interesting, they could simply take whatever parts they wanted and ask Ubuntu or another distribution to integrate it. The community and the developer would get nothing. The project would have no leverage, no future, and no sustainability.

Under PolyForm Noncommercial, if a company finds value in this project, they have to come to the table. That opens up real possibilities:

- The developer can negotiate fair terms.
- Contributors could become paid contributors.
- The project can sustain itself and keep growing.

**None of this would be possible under GPL.**

Think of it this way: PolyForm Noncommercial doesn't remove any freedom from the community. It adds the possibility of a future where the people who build this project can actually be supported for their work.

## What This Makes Possible

In the open source world, there's a well-known problem: if a company invests in improving a GPL project, their competitors receive those improvements for free. This means there is little incentive for any single company to invest significantly in an independent GPL project — the return simply isn't there.

PolyForm Noncommercial changes that equation. Because commercial rights require a license agreement, it becomes possible for a company to partner with the project and gain a genuine competitive advantage from that partnership. A hardware vendor could sponsor development of features tailored to their platform, knowing that the result isn't automatically available to every competitor. That kind of partnership funds real development, creates jobs, and moves the project forward in ways that pure GPL never could.

This isn't a restriction — it's what makes real investment in independent projects viable.

## Being Honest About the Odds

I'm not building Nordix because I expect it to become the next big distribution. I'm a solo developer, and the odds of any independent project attracting commercial interest are slim.

But if there is even a small chance — I don't want to have already given that chance away. This license is not about restricting the community. It's about keeping a door open that GPL would permanently close.

## The Bottom Line

> **Don't see PolyForm Noncommercial as a barrier for the community.
> See it as the only realistic path where independent developers
> and their contributors can be recognized and supported
> if the work they do turns out to matter.**
